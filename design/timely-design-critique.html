The ethos I'm designing toward

Five beliefs the best apps of 2026 are built on.

These aren't decoration trends — they're the load-bearing principles behind Apple's Liquid Glass system, the calm-design movement, and the way modern fitness apps make dense data feel effortless. Every critique below is measured against them.

01

Content leads, chrome floats

The data is the interface. Controls sit on translucent layers above the content, never competing with it. Hierarchy comes from depth — elevation, blur, soft shadow — not from drawing another box with another border.

02

One screen, one job

Each view has a single primary action that is large, obvious, and lands where the thumb already is. Everything else is secondary and quiet. A wall of equal-weight buttons is a decision tax paid on every visit.

03

Glanceable first, deep on demand

An athlete reads their app mid-stride. The one number that matters today should be legible in half a second. Detail is earned through progressive disclosure — tap to go deeper, never front-load the dashboard.

04

Calm by subtraction

Cognitive ease beats feature density. Fewer competing colors, generous breathing room, restrained type scale. If a label, border, or chip isn't doing real work, it's noise — remove it.

05

Motion that means something

Transitions explain where things came from and where they go. Tactile, physical feedback on every commit. Motion is a continuous living space, not a slideshow of static pages — but it's purposeful, never gratuitous.

Screen 01 · the one you open every day

Today

This is the screen that decides whether Timely feels like a coach or a control panel. Right now it's a vertical stack of up to ten equal-weight panels — countdown, coach card, readiness, the workout, a rating widget, the week report — each in its own bordered box, all shouting at the same volume. There's no answer to the only question a runner has when they open it: what am I doing today, and am I ready?

The real stack: hero → stats → week strip → countdown → coach card → workout → rating → report card. Eight boxes deep before the fold ends.

  • 1No focal point — everything is a box of equal weight.

    Up to ten bordered panels stack vertically, each with the same fill, same 1px border, same radius. The eye has nowhere to land. The single most important thing — today's run — is the fourth or fifth card down.

    Fix → promote one hero. Today's session becomes a full-width, elevated card that owns the top of the screen. Everything else demotes to quiet support.

  • 2The countdown and stat tiles say the same thing twice.

    "128 days to go" appears in a stat tile and the countdown panel. "Mi this week 18/26" duplicates the report-card progress bar. Redundancy is cognitive cost with no payoff.

    Fix → one countdown, one weekly-progress indicator, each shown once.

  • 3Five competing accent colors, no semantic discipline.

    Mint, coral, blue, amber, red all appear above the fold. When everything is colored, color stops meaning anything. The mint accent is also the "today" dot, the active tab, the primary button, and the readiness state — four jobs for one color.

    Fix → mint = "your action / on-track" only. Workout-type colors live as small dots, not full bars.

  • 4The wordmark + race line is wasted real estate.

    "timely · Run on time · MCM Oct 25, 2026 · sub-3:25" is branding on a screen the user opens twenty times a week. They know the app's name. That row should be working — it's prime glance space.

    Fix → collapse to a slim glass bar; surface today's readiness state there instead.

  • 5Rating + shoe picker sits inline, cold, before the run is even done.

    "How did today's 5.0 mi feel?" with a row of five identical buttons is a form, not a moment. It appears with no celebration of the run just completed.

    Fix → move logging into a warm, focused moment (see Screen 03), triggered after the run.

  • ✓What's genuinely good: the readiness data is gold.

    RHR, sleep, Body Battery, HRV, form, weather-adjusted pace — this is real coaching intelligence most apps don't have. The problem is purely presentation, not substance. Don't lose it; frame it.

Eight controls and a 5-item legend eat the top third. Month view (not shown) shrinks to 76px cells with truncated chips — unreadable on a phone.

  • 1The control bar is a cockpit, not a phone screen.

    List/Month, prev/next arrows, Vacation, Sync to Calendar, refresh, and a five-swatch legend wrap across two-plus rows. These are desktop affordances crammed onto 360px. On mobile the buttons fall below the 44pt tap minimum and wrap unpredictably.

    Fix → keep only the List/Month toggle visible. Vacation, Sync, refresh move to a single "···" sheet. The legend becomes contextual — colors are self-evident from the dots.

  • 2Month view doesn't survive the small screen.

    A 7-column grid at phone width gives ~46px cells. Chips truncate, the mileage subtitle is hidden entirely (.chip .mi{display:none}), and drag-to-move — a lovely desktop interaction — is near-impossible with a thumb.

    Fix → make List the default and the star. Reframe Month as a compact heat-strip overview, with full editing reserved for the (better-suited) List.

  • 3The week's hardest signal — planned vs actual — hides on the far right.

    "✓ 3.7" in 12px is the payoff of the whole screen, yet it's the smallest, rightmost element. The week's total mileage progress isn't visualized at the week level at all in List.

    Fix → give each week a header card with a mileage progress ring; align the actual result as a clear status pill on each row.

  • 4The hero block repeats on every tab.

    The wordmark + three stat tiles render identically on Today, Plan, and Activities. That's three copies of branding and duplicated stats, pushing real content down on every screen.

    Fix → a slim shared glass header; let each tab's content start near the top.

  • ✓What's genuinely good: the List logic is the soul of the app.

    Week grouping, per-day color coding, planned-vs-actual judgment, "missed — tap to reschedule" — this is exactly what a marathoner wants. It just needs room to breathe and a clearer visual rhythm.

Screen 03 · the moment after a run

Log how it felt

Logging effort is the data that powers all of Timely's coaching — so the moment deserves care. Today it's a thin slider stranded at the bottom of a long analysis sheet, and in your recording the save actually failed with the words Save failed: [object Object] shown to the user. That single string undoes a lot of trust. This is the highest-leverage screen to make feel premium.

⚠ Save failed: [object Object]

Straight from your recording: a slider, a placeholder note, and a save that errored with [object Object] — the raw, unhandled error shown to the runner.

  • 1"Save failed: [object Object]" is a trust-killer — and a quick fix.

    The toast reads e.message (good instinct) — but here that message is itself an object, almost certainly a {error:{…}} server payload coerced into the Error string. So the runner sees implementation guts at the exact moment they tried to give you data, and their note may be lost.

    Fix → normalize server errors to a string at the fetch boundary, show a human line ("Couldn't save — tap to retry"), keep their input in the field, and add an offline queue so a flaky connection never loses a log.

  • 2A slider is the wrong control for a 1–10 feeling.

    Sliders demand fine motor precision for a coarse, emotional judgment. The current value is invisible until you decode a tiny label underneath. There's no tactile reward, no sense of "I picked Hard."

    Fix → five large effort segments (Recovery → Max), each tappable with a satisfying selected state and a haptic tick. Coarse input, coarse control.

  • 3The rating is stranded at the bottom of a 1,000px analysis sheet.

    To rate a run the user scrolls past route, splits, lap bars, HR zones, and charts. The single action you most want them to complete is the least reachable thing in the sheet.

    Fix → when a run needs a rating, open to the rating moment first; analysis lives one swipe deeper.

  • 4No acknowledgment of the run they just finished.

    The sheet is cold — a form, not a moment. After 5 hard miles the app should reflect the effort back before asking for input.

    Fix → headline the distance, pace, and a one-line read ("Solid easy run — right on target") so logging feels like a reward, not a chore.

  • 5Two near-identical rating UIs exist.

    Today uses 1–2/3–4/… band buttons; the run sheet uses a 1–10 slider. Same concept, two designs, two mental models. Inconsistency taxes the user.

    Fix → one effort component everywhere it appears.

Zooming out

The system, and where I'd start.

The individual screens share a small set of root causes. Fix these at the system level and every screen improves at once.

A

Build a depth scale, retire the boxes

Replace "every section = bordered panel" with three elevation levels: base content, raised cards (soft shadow, no border), and floating glass (blurred, for headers/sheets). Hierarchy by depth, not outline.

B

Give color a job description

Mint = your action / on-track. Coral = coach voice. Workout-type colors = small dots only. Amber/red = warnings only. One semantic system, applied everywhere.

C

Ship a type scale

Today there are ~12 ad-hoc font sizes set inline. Define six steps (Display, Title, Headline, Body, Caption, Micro) and ban inline sizes. Instant consistency and rhythm.

D

Kill the per-tab hero

One slim, shared glass header. Stats live where they're relevant (week progress on Today/Plan), not triplicated as a branding band on every tab.

E

Make errors humane

No raw error objects, ever. Catch, translate, preserve user input, and queue writes offline. "[object Object]" should be impossible by construction.

F

One effort component, with motion

A single tactile RPE control used in every place a run is rated, with haptics and a spring on select. Consistency plus a moment of delight on the action you most want completed.

If I could only ship three things this month

1 — Fix the save error and the rating moment (Screen 03). Highest trust-per-hour: stop showing [object Object], preserve notes, and replace the slider with tactile effort segments.

2 — Promote one hero on Today (Screen 01). Collapse the stack so "what's my run, am I ready" is answered in half a second.

3 — Introduce the depth + type + color system (A, B, C). A small set of tokens that every screen inherits — the cheapest way to make the whole app feel designed rather than assembled.

Scope held to a mobile web PWA throughout — every pattern here (backdrop-blur glass, soft-shadow cards, sticky headers, bottom sheets, CSS transitions, the Vibration API for haptics) ships in mobile Safari today. Nothing here requires going native.
